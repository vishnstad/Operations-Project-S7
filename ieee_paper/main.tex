\begin{abstract}
Land is fixed, yet crops differ sharply in yield, and land is usually allocated by habit
rather than by comparing district-level yield records. This paper formulates crop-area
allocation as two linear programs (LP): maximising total output for a fixed land budget, and
minimising the land needed for a production target. All coefficients are taken from the
Government of India district-wise, season-wise crop production record. Before modelling, a
duplicate copy of 212{,}373 records inflated $100\times$ is found and removed, and crops
reported in nuts or bales are kept out of the pooled total. Yields are averaged across years,
and both models are solved with PuLP and cross-checked with SciPy. For Karnataka's Kharif
season (1998--2019), the optimal plan raises output by 21.6\% on the same land, or meets
historical output on 23.9\% less land; the two dual values confirm the primal/dual pairing.
Sensitivity analysis shows that a cautious $\pm10\%$ re-plan still gives about 6\%, and a
comparison of 27 districts gives a median gain of 25.8\%. An interactive web interface runs
both models for any state and season.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
linear programming, crop planning, land allocation, agricultural resource planning,
sensitivity analysis, duality
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
Yield per hectare varies widely across crops, districts and years, and land is limited: one
crop's gain is another's loss. Historical records exist at district--season--year
granularity but are rarely used to re-plan the next season's allocation. This work asks:
\emph{using actual historical yield records, how should land be re-allocated across crops to
get the most out of a fixed area?}

The objectives are to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item use the dataset's own Yield values as coefficients;
  \item formulate crop allocation as an LP in two forms, maximising output and minimising land~\cite{taha2017};
  \item solve both computationally (PuLP and SciPy \texttt{linprog});
  \item compare the optimal allocation with the actual historical allocation.
\end{itemize}

The scope is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a single state and a single season, with yields averaged over several years;
  \item output or land only, not profit or cost, because the dataset has no cost or price data;
  \item only crops reported in the same production unit, pooled into one LP (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review and Research Gap}
% =====================================================================
Table~\ref{tab:lit} summarises the prior studies. LP has been applied to Indian district-level
crop data~\cite{wankhade2012}, an LP plan has beaten a farmer's own plan~\cite{majeke2020},
realistic gains of 3.75--15\% have been reported~\cite{supriya2022}, and paired cost and
revenue models motivate a primal/dual pair of objectives~\cite{paidipati2017,liang2023}.

\textbf{Gap.} All of these studies use a one-time survey of a single district and assume that
prices are known, so they optimise profit directly. None draws its coefficients from a
multi-year, multi-district government production record. What is missing is an LP built
entirely from an existing public dataset, so that it is reproducible, and one that stays
honest about what the data lacks.

\textbf{Our positioning.} We build the LP strictly from \emph{area}, \emph{production} and
\emph{yield}, using output maximisation and land minimisation only. Cost and profit are left
out rather than approximated.

\section{Dataset}\label{sec:data}
% =====================================================================
The source is the \emph{District-wise, Season-wise Crop Production Statistics} of the
Ministry of Agriculture \& Farmers Welfare, published on data.gov.in and mirrored on
Kaggle~\cite{moafw}. It has 575{,}879 rows and 8 fields covering all of India at district
level:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{State\_Name}, \texttt{District\_Name}: location of cultivation;
  \item \texttt{Crop\_Year}: 1997 to 2020--21;
  \item \texttt{Season}: Kharif, Rabi, Summer, Whole Year, Autumn or Winter;
  \item \texttt{Crop}: Rice, Wheat, Sugarcane, Banana, Cotton, \ldots;
  \item \texttt{Area} (ha), \texttt{Production} and \texttt{Yield}, where Yield $=$ Production$/$Area.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:sample} shows actual rows. The Coconut row is the reason crops must be
unit-checked: its Production is counted in nuts, not weight, so its Yield is on a completely
different scale from Rice or Arecanut.

\subsection{Why this dataset}
Earlier review feedback asked us not to propose a method the data cannot support, and not to
leave a data quirk undiscovered until it breaks the model. Both checks were applied before
anything was formulated.

\textbf{The data gives us:} Area for the land constraint; Yield as the output coefficient; and
multiple years and seasons, so coefficients are averaged rather than guessed. The record is
government-sourced, district-level and multi-year, not a single snapshot.

\textbf{The data does not give us:} any cost or price field, so no profit or cost objective is
possible.

\subsection{Data quirks and cleaning}
The following quirks were found and handled before modelling (Table~\ref{tab:clean}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Some crops are counted, not weighed: Coconut in nuts, and Cotton, Jute and Mesta in
        bales. They are excluded when crops are pooled into one output total. A crop is also
        excluded if its median yield is more than $250\times$ (or less than $1/250$) of the
        median across crops.
  \item The file is two copies stacked together: 212{,}373 records from row 236{,}378 onward
        repeat earlier records with Production exactly $100\times$ larger (99.4\% of the pairs,
        Fig.~\ref{fig:stacked}). The second block is rescaled and the duplicates dropped.
  \item Rows with zero production (mostly unreported) and aggregate rows such as ``Total
        foodgrain'' are dropped; 3{,}565 rows with spelling variants (e.g.\ Paddy) are merged.
  \item After these fixes, yields match Production in tonnes (rice 1.8--2.8\,t/ha).
  \item 1997 and 2020 are partial years, so the analysis window is 1998--2019.
\end{itemize}

\section{Decision Problems and Formulation}
% =====================================================================
For the crops $c$ grown in the chosen state and season, restricted to crops reported in the
same production unit, let:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $x_c$ be the hectares given to crop $c$ (the decision variables);
  \item $y_c$ be its Yield averaged over the available years;
  \item $a_c$ and $m_c$ be its historical minimum and maximum area.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Coefficients from the data}
Let $P_{c,t}$ and $A_{c,t}$ be the production and area of crop $c$ in year $t$, summed over
the districts of the state, and let $T$ be the number of years. Then
\begin{align}
y_c&=\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \frac{P_{c,t}}{A_{c,t}},\qquad
\bar A_c=\frac{1}{T}\sum_t A_{c,t}, \label{eq:coef}\\
a_c&=\min_t A_{c,t},\qquad m_c=\max_t A_{c,t}. \notag
\end{align}
The historical land and output, $L=\sum_c\bar A_c$ and $D=\sum_c y_c\bar A_c$, are the
default right-hand sides. A crop enters the LP only if it is reported in at least two-thirds
of the years (15 of 22).

\subsection{Optimization 1: maximise agricultural output}
\emph{Q1. Given a fixed land budget $L$, how many hectares per crop maximise total production?}
\begin{align}
\max\ Z=\textstyle\sum_c y_c x_c \quad
\text{s.t.}\ \textstyle\sum_c x_c\le L,\ \ a_c\le x_c\le m_c,\ \ x_c\ge0. \label{eq:o1}
\end{align}

\subsection{Optimization 2: minimise land for a production target}
\emph{Q2. To guarantee a minimum total production $D$, what is the smallest land footprint?}
\begin{align}
\min\ \textstyle\sum_c x_c \quad
\text{s.t.}\ \textstyle\sum_c y_c x_c\ge D,\ \ a_c\le x_c\le m_c,\ \ x_c\ge0. \label{eq:o2}
\end{align}

Table~\ref{tab:models} compares the two decision problems. Both use the same data and the
same $y_c$; only the objective and the binding constraint are swapped, which is the standard
primal/dual pairing for a resource-allocation LP~\cite{taha2017,paidipati2017}.

\begin{table}[!t]
\centering
\caption{The two decision problems}\label{tab:models}
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{1.45cm}p{3.15cm}p{3.15cm}@{}}
\toprule
 & Optimization 1 & Optimization 2\\
\midrule
Decision & $x_c$, hectares per crop & $x_c$, hectares per crop\\
Objective & max $\sum_c y_c x_c$ & min $\sum_c x_c$\\
Binding constraint & Land: $\sum_c x_c\le L$ & Target: $\sum_c y_c x_c\ge D$\\
Bounds & $a_c\le x_c\le m_c$, $x_c\ge0$ & $a_c\le x_c\le m_c$, $x_c\ge0$\\
Output & Optimal hectares and output per crop & Least-land allocation vs.\ history\\
Dual & $\lambda^*$: output per extra ha & $\mu^*$: land per extra tonne\\
Technique & Linear programming & Linear programming\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Structure of the solution}
With a single shared constraint, \eqref{eq:o1} is a continuous knapsack. Every crop first
receives its minimum $a_c$; the remaining land is then filled from the highest-yield crop
downward, each crop up to $m_c$. The crop at which the land runs out is the single
\emph{basic} crop, and its yield equals the land dual $\lambda^*$. Each crop's reduced cost
is $y_c-\lambda^*$: crops above $\lambda^*$ sit at their maximum, crops below it at their
minimum. By the same argument, the target dual of \eqref{eq:o2} satisfies $\mu^*=1/\lambda^*$
when \eqref{eq:o1} is solved at the land that \eqref{eq:o2} uses.

\subsection{Optional policy constraint}
The interface (Section~\ref{sec:tool}) can also require that pulse crops $\mathcal P$ keep at
least a share $s$ of the land used:
\begin{align}
\textstyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal P} x_c \;\ge\; s\sum_c x_c. \label{eq:pulse}
\end{align}
This is a linear constraint, so both models remain LPs. It is off by default ($s=0$), and all
headline results below use $s=0$.

\textbf{Optimization 1.} The plan in Fig.~\ref{fig:opt1}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item raises Potato, Onion, Maize, Rice, Ragi, Jowar and Soyabean to their historical maximum;
  \item holds the lower-yield pulses, oilseeds and millets at their minimum;
  \item leaves Bajra as the basic crop.
\end{itemize}
The value of one extra hectare, the land dual $\lambda^*$, is 0.81\,t, which is Bajra's yield.
PuLP and SciPy give the same optimum.

\textbf{Optimization 2.} Historical output can be produced on 1.46 million ha less land, with
Rice as the basic crop. The dual of the target is $\mu^*=0.3806$\,ha/t. Solving
Optimization~1 at that land gives $\lambda^*=2.627$, and $1/2.627=0.3806$, which confirms the
primal/dual pairing.

\section{Sensitivity and Multi-District Comparison}
% =====================================================================
\textbf{Land budget.} Varying $L$ shows diminishing returns: optimal output rises ever more
slowly, and the value of extra land falls in steps (6.6, 2.9, 1.6, then 0.81\,t/ha) as crops
reach their upper bounds (Fig.~\ref{fig:param}).

\textbf{Bounds.} The gain depends on how far each crop may move. Table~\ref{tab:band} limits
every crop to $\pm p\%$ of its historical area. At $\pm10\%$ the gain is 6.2\% more output or
5.0\% less land, which is consistent with the 3.75--15\% reported in~\cite{supriya2022}.

\begin{table}[!t]
\centering
\caption{Gain when each crop may change by at most $\pm p\%$}\label{tab:band}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
$p$ & 5\% & 10\% & 20\% & 30\% & 50\%\\
\midrule
Output gain & 3.1\% & 6.2\% & 12.5\% & 18.7\% & 31.2\%\\
Land saving & 2.5\% & 5.0\% & 10.0\% & 15.0\% & 25.0\%\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Yields.} Objective-coefficient ranging shows the plan holds while every expanded crop
yields more than $\lambda^*=0.81$\,t/ha. Soyabean is the most fragile: a fall of only
0.05\,t/ha would remove it from the expanded set. A uniform $-30\%$ yield shock only rescales
output and moves no land, while a $-30\%$ shock to Soyabean alone moves 288{,}406\,ha.

\textbf{Pulse share.} The unconstrained plan cuts land under pulses from 23.2\% to 12.6\%.
Adding \eqref{eq:pulse} to keep the historical 23.2\% lowers the gain to 19.0\% more output or
19.1\% less land; a 30\% floor gives 15.1\% or 13.6\%. This quantifies the output cost of
keeping pulses in the crop mix.

\textbf{Districts.} Solving both models separately for each of 27 districts gives a median gain
of 25.8\% more output, or 24.4\% less land (Fig.~\ref{fig:multi}). The gain is highest in the
diverse dry districts (Koppal +65\%, Gadag +55\%, Raichur +48\%) and lowest in the coastal
rice districts (Udupi +0.2\%, Dakshina Kannada +0.6\%). It rises with the number of crops a
district grows ($r=0.50$), and Maize is the crop most often expanded (16 of 27 districts).

\section{Validation}
% =====================================================================
The pipeline was also run on a synthetic dataset of 1{,}664 rows with planted defects:
duplicates, zero-production rows, aggregate rows, spelling variants, crops in nuts and bales,
and a drought year. Every defect was caught. On the government data, the two solvers agree to
within $10^{-9}$, and $\mu^*=1/\lambda^*$ holds as theory predicts.

% =====================================================================
\section{Assumptions and Limitations}
% =====================================================================
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Production units must match.} Crops counted in nuts or bales are excluded
        from the pooled total; this was confirmed from the data, not assumed.
  \item \textbf{Historical averages.} Yields are averaged over years; weather variation is not
        modelled in this base version.
  \item \textbf{Output, not profit.} No cost or price field exists in the dataset, so a tonne
        of onion counts the same as a tonne of pulses, which favours heavy crops.
  \item \textbf{Bounds drive the result.} Historical min/max bounds give +21.6\%; $\pm10\%$
        bounds give +6.2\%.
  \item \textbf{Not modelled:} soil quality, irrigation, labour, crop rotation, and price or
        demand shifts.
\end{enumerate}

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================
Using only area, production and yield from the government record, the two LP models show
that Karnataka's Kharif land could have produced 21.6\% more output, or the same output on
23.9\% less land; even a cautious $\pm10\%$ re-plan gives about 6\%. The dual values of the
two models confirm their primal/dual relationship. Unit checks and duplicate-record checks
applied before modelling were essential to obtaining valid coefficients. Future work can use
price- or nutrition-weighted objectives, add rotation constraints, and build a stochastic LP
from the yield variances already computed.
